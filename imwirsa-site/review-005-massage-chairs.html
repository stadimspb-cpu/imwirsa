When time ashore is short, recovery matters more

EN RU

Southern Ports has installed massage chairs at seafarer centres in Bunbury, Albany and Esperance. On the surface, a modest addition. It raises a much broader question: what should a modern seafarer centre offer when a crew member has only a few hours ashore.

Commentary Issue 005 August 2026

For many seafarers, shore leave today no longer means an extended rest. Port calls are getting shorter, terminals are often far from the city, transport takes time, and the ability to leave port grounds can be limited. In these conditions, even a short spell ashore takes on particular value.

IMWIRSA· Issue 005 · August 2026· 3 min read

What Southern Ports did

Three new massage chairs have been installed at the seafarer centres serving the ports of Bunbury and Esperance (Mission to Seafarers) and Albany (Stella Maris) in southern Western Australia. The idea came through the local Port Welfare Committee — and it is not the port's first step in this direction: Southern Ports has previously supported new buses, bicycles and extra wi-fi capacity for visiting crews.

Seafarer centres traditionally provide essential services: contact with family, transport, food and refreshments, pastoral and spiritual support, practical help, and simply a safe space away from the ship. All of these remain a core part of welfare. But the Southern Ports initiative points to another dimension worth attention: short-term physical and psychological recovery as part of shore-based welfare infrastructure.

The principle behind the chair

A massage chair is just one possible example. The principle behind it matters more: if a seafarer has only one or two hours ashore, welfare infrastructure should help make that time genuinely restorative. That can mean quiet rest areas, comfortable seating, showers where feasible, access to connectivity, opportunities for physical relaxation, open spaces, and other simple measures aimed at recovery from fatigue and prolonged time on board.

Taken together, Southern Ports' measures — from transport and connectivity to massage chairs — trace a meaningful sequence: access ashore → transport → connectivity → rest → recovery.

The IMWIRSA View

IMWIRSA believes the next stage in shore-based welfare does not necessarily require entirely new facilities. In many ports, the priority may instead be strengthening existing seafarer centres and adapting them to the realities of modern port calls — shorter stays, remote terminals, and limited time to go ashore.

So the question for the international seafarer welfare community is much broader than the usefulness of massage chairs. The real question is different: how can existing shore-based welfare infrastructure give a seafarer genuine recovery when time is their scarcest resource? Southern Ports' experience is a small but practical example worth watching.

A. Miller Chair, IMWIRSA Scientific & Advisory Board of Trustees
